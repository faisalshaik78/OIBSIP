Marie Curie

She followed an invisible light and changed how we see matter.

Warsaw, 1867 — Passy, 1934

A life given to the unknown

Maria Skłodowska was born in Warsaw at a time when Poland was under foreign rule and women were barred from university study. Her parents were teachers who valued learning above comfort, and she grew up believing that knowledge was something worth sacrificing for.

She worked as a governess to help pay for her sister's medical studies, then travelled to Paris in 1891 to enrol at the Sorbonne. She lived in a cold attic room, often forgot to eat, and still finished at the top of her class in physics, then earned a second degree in mathematics.

Together with her husband Pierre, she began studying the strange rays given off by uranium. Hours of patient, physically punishing work in a leaky shed led them to two new elements, polonium (named for her homeland) and radium. She coined the word "radioactivity" for the phenomenon and showed it came from within the atom itself, an idea that opened the door to modern physics.

Her life was not only triumph. Pierre's sudden death in 1906 left her a widow with two daughters, yet she took over his professorship and became the first woman to teach at the Sorbonne. During the First World War she helped equip vehicles with X-ray machines and drove to the front herself. She never patented her methods, believing science belonged to everyone. Her example still tells every student that curiosity and persistence can outlast any obstacle.

Nothing in life is to be feared, it is only to be understood.

Marie Curie

Milestones

  1. 1867

    Born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw, the youngest of five children.

  2. 1891

    Moves to Paris to study physics and mathematics at the Sorbonne.

  3. 1898

    Announces the discovery of polonium and radium with Pierre Curie.

  4. 1903

    Shares the Nobel Prize in Physics with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel, becoming the first woman to win a Nobel.

  5. 1906

    After Pierre's death, becomes the first female professor at the Sorbonne.

  6. 1911

    Wins the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, the first person honoured in two scientific fields.

  7. 1914–1918

    Develops mobile X-ray units that help doctors treat wounded soldiers.

  8. 1934

    Dies of an illness linked to long-term radiation exposure, leaving a legacy that transformed science and medicine.

Why she still matters

Two elements found

Polonium and radium expanded the periodic table and proved that atoms are not unchanging.

Two Nobel Prizes

Physics in 1903 and Chemistry in 1911, in two different sciences.

Medicine, front line

Her mobile X-ray units brought imaging to field hospitals and her work underpins cancer radiotherapy.

A door held open

She trained researchers and founded the Radium Institutes in Paris and Warsaw, making space for women in science.

Legacy

Science became more human because of her work

Marie Curie showed that discovery is not only a matter of brilliance but of patience, discipline, and courage. Her research changed medicine, rewrote the understanding of matter, and made clear that the pursuit of knowledge can serve society in profound ways.

She remains a symbol of intellectual freedom: a woman who insisted on learning, even when the world told her she was not meant to belong in the laboratory.

Her enduring influence

  • Founded new standards for scientific rigor and research
  • Helped launch modern cancer treatment through radioactive therapy
  • Inspired generations of women in mathematics, physics, and medicine
