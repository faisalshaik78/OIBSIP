THE WOMAN BEHIND THE DISCOVERY

Curiosity changed
the course of science.

Marie Skłodowska Curie was born in Warsaw in 1867, at a time when opportunities for women in higher education were sharply limited. Determined to continue learning, she eventually moved to Paris, where she studied physics and mathematics at the Sorbonne.

In Paris, she met physicist Pierre Curie. Their shared curiosity became a remarkable scientific partnership. Through painstaking experiments with radioactive materials, Marie helped establish radioactivity as a field of scientific study and investigated the unusual properties of uranium-bearing minerals.

Their research led to the identification of two new elements, polonium and radium. The work demanded patience and persistence: isolating tiny quantities of radioactive substances required years of careful laboratory effort under difficult conditions.

Marie Curie's achievements extended beyond the laboratory. During the First World War, she helped bring mobile X-ray units to medical teams, enabling doctors to examine wounded soldiers. Her legacy includes not only discoveries, but also a lasting example of determination, rigorous inquiry, and service to others.

MILESTONES

A remarkable journey

  1. 1867

    A beginning in Warsaw

    Born Maria Skłodowska in Poland.

  2. 1891

    New chapter in Paris

    Begins studies at the Sorbonne.

  3. 1898

    New elements identified

    Marie and Pierre Curie announce polonium and radium.

  4. 1903

    First Nobel Prize

    Shares the Nobel Prize in Physics for research on radiation.

  5. 1911

    A second Nobel Prize

    Receives the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for her work on radium and polonium.

  6. 1914–18

    Science in service

    Supports wartime medical imaging with mobile X-ray units.
